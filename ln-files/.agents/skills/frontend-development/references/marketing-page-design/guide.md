# Marketing Page Design

Use this skill for public-facing pages where the visitor is deciding whether to trust, inquire, book, buy, visit, apply, or keep reading. Make decisions with attention to copy, information architecture, imagery, visual rhythm, and conversion paths.

## Page Strategy

Decide the single primary message first. Treat other information as proof, method, audience, result, or supplement, and align headings, CTAs, imagery, page routes, and supporting details around that message.

Do not design a top page as a summary of every lower page. Select only the representative routes that support the goal, such as inquiry, booking, visit, purchase, recruitment, or key service pages. Give each section one role and one clear question to answer.

## Page Intent

Before choosing a visual direction, identify whether the page is primarily brand-led or conversion-led.

A brand-led page helps visitors remember, understand, trust, or emotionally locate the business, product, place, or campaign. It can use more distinctive composition, mood, imagery, pacing, and typography when those choices clarify identity or leave a durable impression.

A conversion-led page helps visitors decide and act now. It should make the offer, relevance, proof, objection handling, and CTA path clear. Distinctive visuals are still useful, but they must not hide the action, dilute the offer, or slow comparison.

Many pages need both. Decide which role leads the first viewport, and which role supports later sections.

## Section Title Strategy

Choose section titles by their role and weight inside that specific page, not by fixed content category. The same category can take a plain label, a benefit-led phrase, or a stronger copy line depending on how important it is to the page's main message.

Use direct labels for information the business owns or readers actively look for: pricing, menu, access, staff, results, process, and similar lookup sections. Be more careful with labels that describe the reader's state, emotion, or desire, such as concerns, needs, anxiety, or requests; those can feel like internal sorting or an assumption about the reader. Reframe them as concrete situations, audiences, use cases, or questions.

Default most section titles to short, scannable noun-phrase labels rather than full sentences. In Japanese this means 体言止め (noun-ending phrases), not predicate sentences ending in です・ます or a verb. A page where almost every section title is a sentence reads as monotonous and slow, and buries the few lines that should stand out; convert lookup and method sections back to concise labels.

Use copy-led sentence titles sparingly as accents, and keep them a clear minority of the page's section titles — as a rough guide, no more than about a third. Reserve them for the primary promise (usually the hero) and the main action or CTA, and occasionally for personal relevance or hesitation removal. Treat examples as context checks, not templates: a staff section can be a direct label when it is lookup information, but a stronger copy title when the people are a major reason to choose the service; a concern section usually works better as a concrete situation or question than as a label like "Concerns."

## Copy And Content Role Strategy

Decide what each visible element is responsible for: heading, lead, body text, card, table, stat, image, caption, CTA, or note. Keep an element when it adds a distinct role such as framing the section, helping the reader judge relevance, explaining sequence, reducing hesitation, carrying emotion, or giving necessary evidence.

Avoid stacking multiple elements that do the same job. Do not add a sentence merely to announce, summarize, or repeat information that the adjacent heading, cards, table, numbers, image, or CTA already communicates.

Do not add a small subtitle, eyebrow, kicker, or overline above a heading by default. This small label above the title is a habit, not a requirement, and most headings read better without it. Omit it unless it carries information the heading itself cannot, such as a section number in a sequence, a category the reader needs to place the section, or a label that genuinely changes how the heading is read. When in doubt, drop it and let the heading stand alone. Do not use it to restate the heading, add a generic tagline, or decorate the section.

Concrete information does not have to appear in prose. Choose prose when nuance, trust, atmosphere, or explanation matters; choose structured content such as numbers, labels, lists, tables, cards, captions, or CTAs when comparison, scanning, pricing, steps, results, or specifications matter.

Before keeping or adding a copy element, check whether it has a role nearby elements do not, helps the reader decide or act, and avoids repeating visible information without adding meaning.

Write copy that a first-time visitor can judge quickly. Prefer reader-facing wording over abstract slogans, internal categories, or copy that could apply unchanged to any business. Ground claims in supplied evidence; do not invent rankings, reviews, guarantees, prices, qualifications, or outcomes.

Use CTA labels that describe the visitor's action. Avoid repeating the same meaning, phrase, claim, CTA, card pattern, or section role.

## Direction Exploration

When the page has no fixed visual reference, explore 2-4 distinct directions before producing the final layout. Do not treat color changes, border radius changes, or decorative accents as separate directions.

Make alternatives differ by page intent and structure. Vary at least three of these: first-view composition, section order, typography scale, image treatment, proof material, CTA placement, background structure, motion, and content density.

For each direction, state the first viewport, section order, visual device, CTA strategy, and tradeoff. Choose the final direction based on audience, content, page intent, implementation constraints, and maintainability.

## Layout Variety And Visual Focus

Do not default to a card grid. First decide what visual form best fits the content's job. Use cards when items are truly peer-level and need comparison. For other jobs, consider forms that match the content: bubbles or scattered short lines for anxieties and quotes; horizontal lists for compact proof; comparison tables or side-by-side blocks for choices; timelines, arrows, or stepped bands for process; large photos with captions for evidence or atmosphere; split layouts for contrast; or sparse text-only blocks when the message should feel direct.

Give each viewport a clear visual lead. Avoid placing many equally loud boxes, badges, CTAs, stats, and links in one screen. Choose the primary action or idea first, then make secondary information smaller, quieter, grouped, or spatially separate.

Treat "neat but dull" as a marketing-page design failure. A section can be aligned and readable but still too predictable. Add variation through hierarchy, scale, asymmetry, image relationships, typographic contrast, section rhythm, or interaction only when it clarifies priority, supports the message, or keeps the reader moving.

Choose a visual direction that supports the primary message, not just a surface style. Meaningful variation should affect content priority, first-view composition, photography or visual motif, typography, spacing, proof material, CTA framing, or tone.

## Visual Quality

Commit to a clear aesthetic direction and execute it with precision. Use distinctive typography, a cohesive palette, contextual imagery, and background details that fit the subject. Bold maximalism and refined minimalism both work; the key is intentionality, hierarchy, and fit.

Avoid generic AI-generated aesthetics such as interchangeable layouts, predictable card stacks, overused fonts, cliched gradients, and decorative effects that do not support the message.

## References And Assets

When visual direction is open, inspect real reference sites before designing. Prefer galleries that expose categories, tags, screenshots, and live-site links in a way that can be searched and verified.

For Japanese marketing pages, start with SANKOU!, Web Design Clip, MUUUUU.ORG, and I/O 3000. For overseas landing-page structure or visual range, use Lapa Ninja and Siteinspire. Open the original live site for selected references and check first viewport, section order, mobile behavior, imagery, CTA placement, and motion.

Use commercially usable assets proactively when supplied brand assets are insufficient. Consider photos, illustrations, textures, patterns, icons, and hand-drawn materials. Record the source URL and license or usage condition for any asset used. Do not use assets when the license is unclear, requires unavailable attribution, includes recognizable brands in a misleading way, or implies endorsement by identifiable people.
